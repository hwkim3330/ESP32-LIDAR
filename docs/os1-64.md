# The 64 beam sensor does not fit through a W5500

The OS1-16 worked here for a day. Its replacement, an **OS-1-64**, does not, and the reasons are
structural rather than a matter of tuning.

## Why it fragments, and why that is fatal

A column is 12 bytes of header plus four per pixel. Sixteen beams make a 76 byte column; sixty-four
make 268. The sensor will not send fewer than sixteen columns in a packet —

```
columns_per_packet must be a positive multiple of 16
```

— so the smallest datagram an OS1-64 emits is `32 + 16*268 + 32 = 4352` bytes, which is three IP
fragments on any 1500 byte link. There is no setting that avoids it.

And this board cannot receive a fragmented datagram at all:

```
# CONFIG_LWIP_IP4_REASSEMBLY is not set        (Arduino ESP32 libraries)
#define IP_REASSEMBLY 0                        (lwipopts.h)
```

**Reassembly is compiled out.** Fragments are discarded on arrival, always, at any rate. The
symptom is a saturated wire and a socket that reads zero, and narrowing the azimuth window changes
how many arrive rather than whether they survive — measured at 45°, 90°, 180° and 360°, all zero.

**This also corrects an earlier conclusion.** Yesterday the OS1-16 at `1024x10` with the full
profile — 3392 byte datagrams, also fragmented — produced the same saturated-wire-zero-packets
symptom, and it was written up as the board being overwhelmed by fragment rate. It was not. Those
datagrams were being discarded on arrival for exactly this reason, and no amount of slowing them
down would have helped.

## Reassembling them here works, but costs the rest of the stack

`reassemble.h` takes the driver's input path, puts the sensor's fragments back together, and hands
completed datagrams to the same decoder a socket would have fed. It works: **25,039 fragments
became 8,345 datagrams**, three to one as expected, two abandoned, with a flat heap.

Then everything else stops. With the input path taken over, lwIP receives nothing — the switch
stops answering CoAP, the sensor stops answering HTTP, and about thirty seconds later the sensor's
ARP entry for this board expires and even the stream being reassembled dies. The passthrough for
frames this code does not consume is wrong somewhere, and until it is right the feature costs more
than it buys. It is behind `R` on the console rather than on by default.

## The second core does its half perfectly; the chip does not

Decoding was moved off the driver's thread — the callback now copies a datagram into one of two
slots and swaps, and a task pinned to the other core unpacks it. That half works completely:
**zero abandoned, zero dropped**, the decoder never once behind at 320 datagrams a second.

It changes nothing about the failure. With reassembly running the board takes the stream cleanly
for **twelve to fourteen seconds** — 3,900 datagrams from 11,700 fragments, heap flat — and then
stops dead, every time, at every azimuth window tried:

| window | datagrams | how long it lasted | SPI errors |
|---|---|---|---|
| 180° | 4,632 at 93/s | 14 seconds of 47 | 23 |
| 360° | 3,900 at 320/s | 12 seconds | 31 |

What stops it is the chip — but not in the way I first wrote it. Two corrections to that entry:

**It is bursty, not permanent.** The driver recovers. Errors come at 6 s, 30 s, 40 s, and between
them it polls the link and takes packets normally. "Wedged forever" was wrong.

**It is not the average rate.** Narrowing the sensor to a 90 degree window -- 2.8 Mbit/s, a third
of what this same chip *transmits* for three minutes without one error -- still kills it. So the
lever I reached for does not work, and the reason it does not is the more useful finding.

What is left is the shape. An OS1-16 sent one 1500 byte frame every 3125 µs. An OS1-64 sends a
4352 byte datagram, which is **three full frames back to back at line rate**, and the W5500
overflows on the burst rather than on the hour. Averages are the wrong unit for this part.

The first burst arrived at 6 s, when the sensor was not sending to this board at all -- its
udp_dest was still a stale address from an earlier session. The switch does not know that MAC, so
it floods those frames to every port, and the ESP eats a storm addressed to somebody else. **A
small endpoint on a shared switch cannot opt out of traffic it is not the destination of.**

The raw errors:

```
w5500_spi_read(166): spi transmit failed
emac_w5500_receive(750): read payload failed, len=1434, offset=48102
```

**48102 is past the end of a 16 kB receive buffer.** The buffer overflows because the driver
cannot drain 1500 byte frames over SPI as fast as they arrive, the read pointer desynchronises,
and from that moment the driver is wedged permanently — it retries the same impossible read
forever, and lwIP receives nothing either. Narrowing the window to 180° halves the rate and buys
two more seconds.

So transmit and receive are not symmetric on this part. Eight megabits a second of 1500 byte
frames go **out** without a single FCS error over three minutes; a third of that coming **in**
overflows it in twelve seconds. Sending is the chip's own buffer emptying at line rate; receiving
is the host having to keep up, and the host is a serial bus.

## And the SPI does not survive this traffic either

Under the OS1-64's stream the driver reports its own trouble:

```
w5500_spi_read(166): spi transmit failed
emac_w5500_receive(750): read payload failed, len=1434, offset=48102
```

Continuously at 40 MHz; **17 errors in 81 seconds at 20 MHz**. Yesterday's stability measurement —
three minutes at 40 MHz, 117,562 frames, not one corrupt — was taken with 8 Mbit/s of *transmit*
and a small, unfragmented receive. This is a different load, and the clock that was right for the
old sensor is not right for this one. The SPI clock is not a property of the board alone.

## What this means for the vehicle

For the SCOUT MINI build, **the LiDAR should go straight to the Jetson**, which has real gigabit
and a stack that reassembles. A W5500 on an ESP32-S3 tops out around 8 Mbit/s of unfragmented
traffic; an OS1-64 at its slowest setting is 11 Mbit/s of fragmented traffic. It is the wrong end
of the wire for this sensor.

That does not remove the board from the design — it moves it. The ESP and the LAN9662 belong in
the path as the TSN element: shaping, gating and measuring the traffic that shares the network with
the sensor, which is what the last two days measured them doing well. What they are not is the
sensor's receiver.

## Five things that did not fix it

Each of these was a real hypothesis with a mechanism behind it, tested on the bench, and each is
now closed. Recorded so nobody spends the afternoon again.

| tried | reasoning | result |
|---|---|---|
| decode on the second core | the input callback was doing 1024 pixels of work per datagram | perfect -- 0 dropped, 0 abandoned, and no change to the failure |
| narrow the azimuth window | fewer packets is less load | 2.8 Mbit/s dies the same way as 11 |
| leaky-bucket shaper on the switch | space the three frames of a datagram apart | shaper is inert: 11 Mbit/s passes a 1 Mbit/s limit on all eight classes |
| force the link to 10 Mbit | the wire itself becomes the pacer -- 1.2 ms per frame | port 1 refuses 10 Mbit (CoAP 4.05); it is a 100 Mbit-only port |
| SPI at 40 MHz instead of 20 | halve the time to drain each frame | dies at the same point |
| PSRAM out of the DMA path | a buffer in PSRAM would explain "fine, then suddenly never" | refuted directly: every frame buffer reads `0x3fcf....`, internal RAM |

The last one is worth keeping as a method. Rather than reasoning about whether the allocator might
be handing out PSRAM, the driver's own buffer address was printed: `frame buffer #2000 at
0x3fcf7160 (internal)`. One line, and a plausible theory was dead in a minute.

## What is actually left

Not the sensor, not the switch, not the CPU, not the clock, not memory. What remains is the
ESP-IDF W5500 driver's receive path itself, which reads a frame in one long SPI transaction and
has no recovery when the chip's read pointer and its own disagree. Fixing that means not using it
-- reading the socket registers and draining the buffer directly, the way the boot-time probe
already talks to the chip before the driver takes over.

That is a real piece of work and it may still lose, because the underlying budget is unforgiving:
at 20 MHz a 1514 byte frame is 605 µs of SPI, and three of them per datagram at 320 datagrams a
second is 58% of every second spent inside SPI transfers before any protocol work at all. The
part is sold as 100 Mbit because its MAC and PHY are; the SPI bus in front of them is not.

## Recovering instead of preventing

Since none of the six prevented the overflow, the last thing tried was surviving it. A watchdog
counts everything arriving -- lidar packets, IMU packets, fragments -- and when all of them stop
for two seconds while the link is up, it restarts the MAC; after ten failed restarts it reboots
the board.

It works, and it is not enough. Measured over 280 seconds:

    30 restarts, 3 reboots, delivering 32% of the time

So the board no longer dies at fifteen seconds and stay dead, which is what it did this morning.
It now cycles: about a minute and a half of stream, then a stall, some restarts, sometimes a
reboot, then stream again. A third of a 1 Hz point cloud still draws a room. It is not something
to build a demonstration on.

Two things were learned making it, both cheap and both worth keeping:

**The deeper restart is unavailable.** Tearing the driver down and rebuilding it -- stop,
del_netif_glue, driver_uninstall, all ESP_OK -- ends with `esp_eth_mac_new_w5500` returning NULL,
because the SPI device the old MAC owned is never released. After that the board has no MAC at
all, which is worse than a wedged one. So the shallow restart is the only one, and the escalation
is a reboot.

**Re-registering the input path is not optional.** `esp_eth_update_input_path` is lost with the
driver, so after a restart fragments go back to lwIP, which drops them. The IMU keeps arriving
through the normal path, the watchdog sees traffic and goes quiet, and the lidar stream is gone
for good while everything looks healthy. Two runs were lost to that before the hook was made
unconditional.

## It was never an overflow

Every diagnosis above assumed the chip's receive buffer was overflowing. It is not. The failing
reads were collected and their positions computed against the 16 kB buffer:

| len | offset | offset & 0x3FFF | end | |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1514 | 48202 | 15434 | 16948 | past the end |
| 1514 | 32142 | 15758 | 17272 | past the end |
| 1434 | 15486 | 15486 | 16920 | past the end |
| 1514 | 15014 | 15014 | 16528 | past the end |
| 1434 | 49002 | 16234 | 17668 | past the end |
| 1514 | 32654 | 16270 | 17784 | past the end |
| 1514 | 15094 | 15094 | 16608 | past the end |

**Twelve of twelve, across two runs, every one straddling the end of the buffer.** In-buffer
offsets between 15014 and 16270 -- the last kilobyte and a half -- and a read that begins anywhere
else has never once failed. This is a wrap-around, not a capacity problem, and it is positional
rather than statistical: a frame that happens to land near the boundary kills the driver, and the
one before it, of the same size at the same rate, does not.

That explains what the load experiments could not. Narrowing the azimuth window did not help
because it changes how often a frame arrives, not where it lands. The second core did not help
because the read fails before any decoding. A shaper would not have helped either. And it explains
why restarting the MAC sometimes recovers: the reset moves the read pointer somewhere else.

The obvious suspect -- a split read exceeding the SPI transfer limit -- was tested by raising
`max_transfer_sz` from its 4092 byte default to 20000. Delivery went from 32% to 42% of the time
over four minutes, which is not enough separation to call a fix, and the failures continue. The
setting is kept because it is harmless, not because it is proven.

What this changes is where the remaining work points. Replacing the receive path is no longer a
vague "write our own driver" -- it is one specific behaviour to get right: read the socket
registers, and when a frame straddles the end of the buffer, split it into two transactions at
the boundary. Everything else the IDF driver does is fine.

## The failing read, exactly

Our own SPI layer went in under the driver -- `eth_spi_custom_driver_config_t`, a hook the driver
already provides, so no fork -- to split reads at the buffer boundary. It did not fix anything,
and the reason it did not is the most precise thing this bench has produced:

    spi read failed: cmd 0x00004000 (16384) addr 0x18 len 92
    spi read failed: cmd 0x00004000 (16384) addr 0x18 len 700
    spi read failed: cmd 0x00004000 (16384) addr 0x18 len 700

**Correction, written after checking how it was measured.** The claim below is weaker than it
reads. The print stopped after four lines, and the driver retries a failed read at the same
address forever -- so four identical lines may be one event printed four times rather than four
events agreeing. And the lengths here (92, 700) never match the ones the driver's own log reports
(1434, 1514), which means the two were watching different failures. Both are now fixed: only the
first occurrence of each distinct (offset, length) is printed, with the eight reads that preceded
it.

**Every failing read is at offset 0x4000 exactly** -- 16384, one past the last address a 16 kB
buffer has -- and the lengths are small, 92 and 700, not the 1514 byte payloads. So it is not a
long read straddling the end. It is one specific pointer value.

That is why the split never fired: `0x4000 & 0x3FFF` is 0, so the straddle test was false. The
earlier "twelve of twelve wrap" table is still true and was still the right thread to pull -- it
is what led here -- but the mechanism is narrower than it looked.

It also fits the timing. The socket read pointer is free-running; landing exactly on 0x4000 is
about one chance in 16384 per frame, and at 960 frames a second that is once every seventeen
seconds. The board dies between twelve and twenty-six.

Masking the offset into the buffer -- `cmd & 0x3FFF`, which turns 0x4000 into the 0 where the
data actually is -- was the obvious fix and measured **worse**: 11% of the time delivering against
48% without it. So it is reverted. The chip is left to fold its own pointer.

The numbers across the day, with the caveat that they move a lot between runs and only the ends
of the range are far enough apart to trust:

| build | delivering |
|---|---|
| restart watchdog only | 32% |
| + max_transfer_sz 20000 | 42% |
| + custom SPI layer with boundary split | 48% |
| + offset masked into the buffer | 11% |

What is left to try is narrow now, which is worth something after a day of broad guesses: catch
the 0x4000 case specifically and re-issue that one read differently -- as two reads, or after
nudging RX_RD, or simply retried -- rather than masking every read. That is a small experiment
rather than a rewrite.

## What the rest of the world knows about this

Searched before spending more bench time, because 0x4000 is too round a number to be nobody
else's problem.

**It is not a known bug.** The W5500 driver has been split out of ESP-IDF into its own component,
[espressif/esp-eth-drivers](https://github.com/espressif/esp-eth-drivers). The current release,
2.0.0 of 2026-06-25, changes nothing here: its changelog is a config-struct refactor and a fix for
poor performance in 10M mode.

**The symptom is common and the cause is not published.** Four ESP-IDF issues describe this exact
cascade -- `spi transmit failed`, then `read RX buffer failed`, then `read payload failed` --
[#6572](https://github.com/espressif/esp-idf/issues/6572),
[#11295](https://github.com/espressif/esp-idf/issues/11295),
[#11845](https://github.com/espressif/esp-idf/issues/11845),
[#14691](https://github.com/espressif/esp-idf/issues/14691). #6572 is marked resolved internally
with nothing visible about what was done. Where a cause is named at all it is someone else's SPI
device sharing the bus, or an oversized transfer.

**Masking is properly ruled out now, not just measured worse.** WIZnet's own reference driver
passes the raw pointer and lets the chip fold it:

    rx_ptr = ((uint32_t)getSn_RX_RD(sn) << 8) + WIZCHIP_RXBUF_BLOCK(sn);

No mask, no split. So the IDF driver is doing what the vendor's driver does, and yesterday's
masking experiment was working against the canonical behaviour. One noisy run said it was worse;
this says why it was always going to be.

**The one published cause does not apply here.** Where those issues name a cause at all, it is
another SPI device sharing the bus. This board does share it -- the boot probe drives the W5500
over Arduino's SPI before handing it to the driver -- so the handover is now reported rather than
assumed:

    W5500 VERSIONR 0x04 (expect 0x04)
    spi bus init: ESP_OK

`spi_bus_initialize` returning ESP_OK means the bus was genuinely free; ESP_ERR_INVALID_STATE
would have meant the probe never released it. So the only explanation anyone has published for
this failure is not ours.

**Emulation is not available.** QEMU has an ESP32-S3 but no W5500 model, so the one part under
suspicion is the one part that would not be there.

## Fixed

    360s: 0 restarts, 0 reboots, delivering 100% of the time
    118,670 datagrams, a steady 320 a second, 32,716 folded reads, 17,106 bounced

Six minutes at the sensor's full rate without a stall, against twelve to twenty-six seconds
before. The cause was a real bug in the ESP-IDF W5500 driver, and it is small:

1. When a frame straddles the end of the 16 kB receive buffer, the driver reads it in two parts.
2. The second part lands at `rx_buffer + (length of the first part)`.
3. That length is **always 2 modulo 4** -- necessarily, because a frame begins immediately after
   its own two byte header. 1370, 1098, 1006, 1054, 1214, 214: every one.
4. So the destination is never word aligned, and the ESP32 SPI master will not DMA into an
   unaligned buffer. It refuses the transaction. The driver has no recovery, and the receive path
   is finished until something resets the MAC.

The fix is to land those reads somewhere aligned and copy, which is `gBounce` in `w5500_spi.h`.
The address fold above it -- the second half being asked for at 16384 rather than 0 -- is kept,
but it was not the decisive part: folding alone reached 54% of the time delivering, and bouncing
took it to 100%.

**Why nobody has hit this.** It needs a frame to land across the buffer end, which for 1500 byte
frames in a 16 kB buffer is about one pass in eleven. A sensor sending 960 full frames a second
gets there in seconds. A browser, a REST client, an MQTT session never does. Every one of the six
things tried before this -- the second core, the azimuth window, the switch shaper, the 10 Mbit
link, the SPI clock, PSRAM -- was aimed at load, and load was only ever how quickly the board
arrived at a fixed one-in-eleven event.

**What found it** was printing the eight reads before each distinct failure rather than reasoning
about which of them was to blame. The pattern was in the first sample:

    cmd  15012  addr 0x18  len     2      the frame header
    cmd  15014  addr 0x18  len  1370      first half, ending at exactly 16384
    cmd  16384  addr 0x18  len   144      second half -- fails

1370 + 144 = 1514. So does 1054 + 460, 1098 + 416, 1006 + 508, 1214 + 300.
